\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

A toxicity classifier is retrained after a new batch of assay results
lands.  Aggregate accuracy is unchanged, yet predictions flip class
for $8\text{--}17\%$ of candidate molecules---some already in
synthesis queues.  We call this \emph{cross-sample prediction
fragility}: the fraction of test predictions that change class
between two models trained on independent bootstraps of the same
population.

Chemistry machine learning has built architectures around invariances
--- to atom indexing, rotation, conformational degrees of freedom
--- and architectures that respect them are now standard.  A further
invariance has gone unspoken: that a model's prediction on a given
molecule should not depend on which subset of the available data the
model trained on.  In a deployment-driven domain where training sets
evolve continuously, this \emph{cross-sample invariance} is plausibly
the property that most directly governs whether a deployed prediction
can be trusted at the next retraining.

Cross-sample fragility is motivated theoretically by algorithmic
stability~\citep{TODO_bousquet2002}, large empirically in small-$N$
scientific machine learning, and missed by the standard uncertainty
toolkit---deep ensembles vary model parameters at fixed data and
remove only ${\sim}2$pp of churn on average.  We measure cross-sample
fragility on eight standard chemistry benchmarks (BACE, BBBP, three
materials-science datasets, three TDC ADME/Tox benchmarks), then
introduce two methods that reduce it under a strict no-test-tuning
protocol: $K$-bootstrap bagging at no accuracy cost, and a
twin-network bootstrap-consistency objective at matched compute.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep0pt
  \item \textbf{Cross-sample prediction fragility} --- the fraction of
        test predictions that change class between two models trained on
        independent bootstraps of the same population --- defines a
        measurable analogue of uniform algorithmic
        stability~\citep{TODO_bousquet2002}.  On eight standard chemistry
        benchmarks (MoleculeNet, TDC ADME/Tox, materials science),
        $8\text{--}17\%$ of test predictions flip across retrainings, an
        order of magnitude larger than deep-ensemble disagreement; deep
        ensembles remove only ${\sim}2$pp of churn.
  \item \textbf{Two methods}, hyperparameters frozen on a single
        development dataset (BACE) and applied unchanged to every
        held-out benchmark.  $K$-bootstrap bagging reduces argmax churn
        $47\text{--}49\%$ at zero accuracy cost.  \textbf{Twin-indep} ---
        two networks on independent bootstraps with a sym-KL consistency
        penalty between heads --- reduces argmax churn $69\text{--}72\%$
        at matched compute against bagging-$K{=}2$, and distributional
        sym-KL by roughly an additional order of magnitude beyond
        bagging.
  \item \textbf{Per-example fragility from one additional bootstrap}
        captures $68\text{--}94\%$ of the predictions that retraining
        will flip in its top decile, giving a deployment-time triage at
        the cost of one extra model.
  \item \textbf{Regime characterisation.}  Twin-indep applies in the
        small-$N$ from-scratch setting that scientific machine learning
        inhabits.  On pretrained-backbone benchmarks (Waterbirds) the
        consistency loss over-regularises features the backbone already
        encodes ($-27$pp accuracy at the chemistry-tuned $\lambda$);
        bagging extends across all settings tested.  Every reported
        delta carries a paired-bootstrap $95\%$ confidence interval over
        seed pairs.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Contribution type (NeurIPS 2026).}
Use-Inspired.  The methods are designed for and evaluated on a specific
real-world setting (small-$N$ from-scratch scientific machine learning,
with deployment-time prediction churn as the practitioner pain).  The
algorithmic-stability connection and the twin-bootstrap-consistency
objective generalise beyond that setting; their honest scope is
documented in Section~\ref{sec:scope}.
