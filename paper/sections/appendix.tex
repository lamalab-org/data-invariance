\section{Theoretical addendum: motivation, not bound}
\label{app:theory}

We use uniform algorithmic stability~\citep{TODO_bousquet2002} only as
motivation for fragility decreasing with $N$, not as a quantitative
bound on the cross-sample fragility $\bar\rho$ we measure.  Three gaps
separate the formal stability framework from the empirical setting of
this paper, and we are explicit about each.

\paragraph{Output-space, not loss-space.}
Stability bounds the change in expected loss when one training example
is replaced.  Argmax disagreement is a non-smooth function of the
prediction distribution: a small change in logits at the decision
boundary can flip the argmax even when the loss change is small.  The
loss-stability bound therefore does not transfer to argmax-stability
without additional assumptions on the margin distribution near the
boundary.

\paragraph{Bootstrap, not single-example perturbation.}
Algorithmic stability is defined for a one-example change between $S$
and $S^i$.  The bootstrap protocol perturbs many examples
simultaneously: a fresh bootstrap differs from the original training
set in roughly $37\%$ of indices, and two independent bootstraps share
only ${\sim}40\%$ of indices in expectation.  Standard stability
results do not give a tight bound under multi-example perturbation, so
$\beta$ alone is not a quantitative predictor of $\bar\rho$ magnitudes
across datasets.

\paragraph{Irreducible boundary disagreement at finite $N$.}
Two ERM models trained on independent samples may differ on examples
that genuinely sit near the population decision boundary, even at
arbitrarily large $N$.  The within-dataset $N$-scaling experiment in
the next section shows the empirical floor.  This floor is not
captured by the $\beta = O(1/N)$ rate, which goes to zero with $N$.

We therefore present cross-sample fragility as a measurable quantity
in its own right (Section~\ref{sec:preliminaries}), with algorithmic
stability serving as the theoretical backdrop that explains why
fragility should decrease with $N$ rather than as a derivation of how
fast.

A plateau of configurations $\lambda \in \{1, 3, 10, 30\}$ all preserve
accuracy while reducing churn modestly; a knee at $\lambda \approx 100$
drops churn substantially; $\lambda{=}300$ extends along the same axis

of the feasible interval that satisfies the $0.02$ accuracy tolerance,
which suggests the rule is not knife-edge: relaxing the tolerance to
$0.01$ would still pick the same $\lambda$, while tightening to
$0.005$ would pick $\lambda{=}100$ (still on the knee).

\section{Within-dataset $N$-scaling on BACE}
\label{app:nscaling}

We isolate $N$ from cross-dataset heterogeneity by subsampling the
BACE canonical training pool to $M \in \{200, 400, 600, 800, 968\}$
and re-running ERM at each $M$ for ten train-seeds.  The pool at each
$M$ is the deterministic prefix of a fixed canonical-seed shuffle, so
all ten train-seeds at the same $M$ draw their bootstraps from the
same underlying pool.  Table~\ref{tab:nscaling} reports cross-pair
sym-KL and argmax churn at each $M$ with paired-bootstrap CIs.

\input{sections/tables/nscaling_bace.tex}

The within-dataset log-log slope is $-0.22$, well below the
theoretical $-1$ predicted by uniform algorithmic stability.  The gap
is consistent with Appendix~\ref{app:theory}: the bootstrap protocol
perturbs many examples simultaneously rather than the single example
that defines $\beta$, and the irreducible floor at finite $N$ (visible
as the plateau between $M=600$ and $M=800$) is not captured by an
$N$-rate that goes to zero.  We therefore do not extrapolate the slope
beyond the measured range.

\section{Design choices we tried and rejected}
\label{app:failures}

We report alternatives we considered before settling on twin-indep
with a fixed $\lambda$ and on independent bootstraps.  All numbers
below are under the headline protocol of
Section~\ref{sec:preliminaries}: ten train-seeds on BACE
($\binom{10}{2} = 45$ paired-bootstrap seed pairs, $10{,}000$
resamples on the seed-pair distribution).

\paragraph{GradNorm-balanced $\lambda$ collapses the consistency loss.}
A parameter-free version of twin-indep would balance the gradient
norms of the consistency and CE losses, replacing the fixed $\lambda$
with an adaptive $\lambda_t$ such that
$\|\nabla \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{cons}}\|
\approx \|\nabla \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CE}}\|$ at every step.
On BACE, this rule drives the effective $\lambda$ to a steady state
of order $1$ within the first three epochs --- two orders of magnitude
below the BACE-frozen $\lambda{=}300$ that we report in the headline
--- and the consistency loss has no measurable effect on argmax
churn (paired $\Delta = -0.1$pp $[-0.5, +0.4]$ vs.\ ERM, $95\%$ CI
includes zero).  The distributional sym-KL between bootstraps is
reduced significantly but only marginally
($\Delta = -0.15$ $[-0.17, -0.13]$); twin-indep at $\lambda{=}300$
reduces sym-KL by an additional order of magnitude.  The interpretation
is mechanical: when the two networks share an architecture and
initialisation distribution, their gradients on the consistency loss
are naturally large because the two heads are not yet specialised, so
GradNorm shrinks $\lambda$ aggressively; the consistency loss must
\emph{dominate} the CE gradient by roughly two orders of magnitude
before churn moves measurably.  We conclude that gradient-norm
balancing is the wrong inductive bias for this objective.

\paragraph{Adversarial partitions agree more, not less.}
We considered replacing independent bootstraps with a learned
worst-case partition: an inner adversary chooses the data split that
maximises prediction disagreement between the two heads, and the outer
model trains to be robust to that split.  In the small-$N$
from-scratch regime this fails to produce divergent twin training.
The adversary tends to assign hard examples consistently to the
\emph{same} head --- because both heads start at the same loss
landscape and any consistent assignment of hard examples specialises
one head more than the other --- which makes the heads agree more, not
less, on average.  Independent bootstraps are the simpler and stronger
choice.

\paragraph{Weight decay alone is insufficient.}
Sweeping AdamW weight decay over five orders of magnitude
($10^{-5}$ to $10^{-1}$) on BACE, BBBP, and TADF produced no detectable
movement in cross-sample churn (paired CIs vs.\ ERM include zero at
every value tested).  The effective per-step regularisation under
AdamW does not constrain the data-resampling sensitivity that drives
churn.

\section{Borderline datasets}
\label{app:borderline}

\paragraph{MOF-thermal.}
Per-method id-acc on MOF-thermal, ten train-seeds:

\begin{itemize}\itemsep0pt
  \item ERM:                       $0.616 \pm 0.013$.
  \item Bagging $K{=}5$:           $0.608 \pm 0.013$.
  \item Distillation-from-anchor:  $0.598 \pm 0.012$.
  \item Twin-indep $\lambda{=}300$: $0.535 \pm 0.005$.
  \item Codistillation:             $0.535 \pm 0.003$.
\end{itemize}

The methods that couple two networks via a strong consistency loss
(twin-indep, codistillation) converge to the constant majority-class
predictor on this dataset.  The methods that do not couple the two
networks (ERM, bagging, distillation-from-a-frozen-anchor) preserve
ERM-level accuracy.  This is the failure mode discussed in
Section~\ref{sec:scope}; we exclude MOF-thermal from
Figure~\ref{fig:main} and Table~\ref{tab:main}.

\paragraph{hERG, HIA\_Hou, Skin\_Reaction.}
These three datasets pass the ERM-versus-majority filter only
marginally ($+3$ to $+4$pp on test sets of $57$--$104$ examples).
Their cross-sample magnitudes appear in
Table~\ref{tab:fragility-magnitudes}; we do not run the headline
method comparison on them because the test sets do not give enough
statistical power.

\section{Predictive entropy as a flip-predictor baseline}
\label{app:entropy}

A natural deployment-cheaper alternative to per-example fragility is
the predictive entropy of a single trained model: the practitioner
already has access to a softmax distribution at deployment time and
need not train a second model.  We test whether the per-example
entropy ranks test predictions for retraining-flip risk as well as
fragility does.

\input{sections/tables/entropy_vs_fragility.tex}

Across the eight headline datasets, fragility captures more of the
total flip-mass in its top decile and has a larger area under the
precision-vs-coverage curve than predictive entropy.  The gap is
consistent and ranges from $1$pp to $13$pp on top-decile recall.  The
practitioner cost of computing fragility is one additional ERM
training run, which the speedup over training a full
$K$-bootstrap-bagging ensemble may amortise; entropy is free at
deployment time but is a meaningfully weaker signal.

\section{Aggregate statistical test (Friedman + Nemenyi)}
\label{app:friedman}

The pairwise CIs reported in Table~\ref{tab:main} test each
(method, dataset) cell separately.  As an aggregate cross-dataset
test, we apply the Friedman non-parametric rank test on mean
cross-sample id-churn over the eight datasets and five methods (ERM,
Deep Ensemble $K{=}5$, Bagging $K{=}2$, Bagging $K{=}5$, Twin-indep
$\lambda{=}300$).  The test rejects the null of equal ranks at
$\chi^2 = 28.10$, $p = 1.19 \times 10^{-5}$.  Mean ranks (lower is
better, $1$ = best on cross-sample id-churn) are
twin-indep $1.50$, bagging $K{=}5$ $1.62$, bagging $K{=}2$ $2.88$,
deep-ensemble $K{=}5$ $4.25$, ERM $4.75$.  The Nemenyi critical
difference at $\alpha{=}0.05$ is $\textit{CD}{=}2.16$; rank gaps
exceeding $\textit{CD}$ identify pairs that differ significantly.
Twin-indep is significantly better than ERM and deep-ensemble; it is
\emph{not} significantly different in rank from bagging $K{=}5$
(rank gap $0.12$).  Twin-indep therefore matches bagging $K{=}5$ in
mean-rank at roughly $2{\times}$ ERM compute
(Appendix~\ref{app:compute}) versus the $5{\times}$ that bagging
$K{=}5$ requires.

\section{Reproducibility}
\label{app:repro}

\paragraph{Canonical-data protocol.}
For every dataset we fix \texttt{canonical\_data\_seed{=}99} once at
construction time.  This single seed determines (i) the train /
in-distribution / scaffold-out split, (ii) the byte-identical canonical
test set used across every method and seed.  Cross-sample variation
comes only from \texttt{train\_seeds=1,2,\dots,10}, which control which
bootstrap of the canonical training pool each model sees.  No method
ever sees a different test set; no method ever uses a different
canonical training pool.

\paragraph{Frozen hyperparameter.}
The single tuned hyperparameter, twin-indep $\lambda$, is selected on
BACE only by the rule ``largest $\lambda \in
\{1, 3, 10, 30, 100, 300\}$ with $\text{id-acc} \ge \text{ERM-id-acc}
- 0.02$''.  This rule yields $\lambda{=}300$
(Figure~\ref{fig:pareto}).  We apply $\lambda{=}300$ unchanged to every
held-out chemistry dataset; no held-out tuning at any stage.

\paragraph{Dataset-by-dataset training hyperparameters.}
All datasets use AdamW with weight decay $10^{-4}$, gradient clipping
at $1.0$, no learning-rate schedule.  Per-dataset learning rate, batch
size, and epoch count are listed in
\texttt{scripts/run\_experiment.py:HPARAMS}; we use $\text{lr}=10^{-3}$,
batch size $64$, and $30$ epochs for the headline chemistry
datasets, and the published Waterbirds recipe ($\text{lr}=10^{-4}$,
batch size $64$, $15$ epochs) for the scope analysis.

\paragraph{Compute footprint.}
The chemistry experiments run end-to-end on a single laptop CPU; the
total compute budget for the small-$N$ portion of the paper is
${\sim}50$ CPU-hours.  Waterbirds (Section~\ref{sec:scope}) runs on a
single A100 GPU with a budget of ${\sim}6$ GPU-hours total.  Each
\texttt{cross\_sample\_train.py} run records reproducibility metadata
(canonical seed, train seed, mode, $\lambda$); all trained
predictions are saved as NPZ.

\paragraph{Reproducing every table and figure.}
Once the NPZ predictions exist under
\texttt{outputs/cross\_sample/\{dataset\}/}, every paper artefact
regenerates with one command:
\begin{verbatim}
make tables       # paper Table 1, fragility-magnitudes table
make figures      # Fig 1 forest, Fig 2 Pareto, Fig 3 decile
\end{verbatim}
To regenerate the predictions from scratch, the same
\texttt{Makefile} provides
\texttt{make sweep-erm}, \texttt{sweep-bagging},
\texttt{sweep-deep-ensemble}, \texttt{sweep-twin-indep}, and
\texttt{sweep-pareto-bace}.  Cluster array scripts that drive these
sweeps in parallel are in \texttt{slurm/}.
