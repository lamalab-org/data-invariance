\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{What practitioners should do.}
For deployed scientific machine learning at $N \lesssim 5000$ from
scratch, run $K$-bootstrap bagging with $K{=}5$ as the default: it
removes roughly half of the per-example retraining churn at a
$5\times$ training cost, with no accuracy regression
(Table~\ref{tab:main}).  When stability matters more than compute,
twin-indep at $\lambda{=}300$ removes the next layer of churn at
$2\times$ ERM training cost, matched against bagging-$K{=}2$.

\paragraph{Cross-sample fragility belongs alongside accuracy.}
Two ERM models with identical aggregate accuracy can disagree on a
fifth of the test set.  None of the standard report columns ---
accuracy, AUROC, calibration, ECE, worst-group accuracy --- captures
that disagreement, because each fixes the dataset and varies something
else (architecture, threshold, group).  Cross-sample fragility is
the column that varies the dataset and fixes everything else, and it
is the column that tracks the deployment quantity practitioners
observe between retrainings.  The clinical-biostatistics
literature~\citep{TODO_riley2023} already reports per-individual
stability intervals as a model-development standard; the same case
extends to scientific machine learning.

\paragraph{Stability is a property of the procedure, not the model.}
A model's churn under retraining is not a fact about the trained
weights; it is a fact about the entire training procedure that
produced them, including the choice of optimiser, schedule, and
data-resampling protocol.  This has three consequences worth naming.

First, the natural target for stability research is the procedure,
not the model.  Twin-indep is one such procedure, but the
bootstrap-overlap analysis (Section~\ref{sec:related}) shows that
even a single hyperparameter on the procedure --- how much data the
two networks share --- makes the difference between catastrophic
failure and the best result we measured.  Procedure design has more
slack than model design at fixed compute.

Second, this changes what reporting standards should look like.
Existing leaderboards report a model card: accuracy, AUROC, sometimes
calibration.  A procedure card would also report churn at fixed-$N$
retraining, with a paired-bootstrap CI.  In a domain where training
sets evolve --- drug discovery, materials discovery, clinical risk
modelling, recommendation systems with continuous data refresh ---
the procedure card is the operationally relevant document.  The
clinical-biostatistics literature~\citep{TODO_riley2023} already
reports per-individual stability intervals; nothing prevents
extending the same to ML benchmarks beyond requiring multiple
retrainings.

Third, the analogy to architectural invariances is informative.
Equivariant networks built rotation, permutation, and translation
invariance into the architecture once and never tuned them again.
Cross-sample invariance is statistical, not architectural, but the
analogy holds: once the bias is in the procedure, it does not have
to be re-derived per model.  We expect bootstrap-aware data
augmentation, subbagging schedules, and training-set diversity
penalties to act on the same axis as twin-indep; whether any of them
match twin-indep at matched compute is open.

The fragility definition extends unchanged to regression (MAE or
covered-interval metric in place of class-flip rate) and to
multi-class; we verify neither.  An $N$-aware scaling
$\lambda_N \propto 1/N$, motivated by the algorithmic-stability rate
but unverified beyond BACE, is the obvious follow-up for removing
the one tuned hyperparameter.
