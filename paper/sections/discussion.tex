\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{What the methods buy.}
The intro's toxicity classifier loses class on $8\text{--}22\%$ of
test molecules between retrainings on independent bootstraps of the
same training population.  Bagging-$K{=}5$ cuts that to
$4\text{--}11\%$ at no accuracy cost ($5\times$-ERM compute,
Table~\ref{tab:main}).  Twin-indep at $\lambda{=}300$ ($2\times$-ERM
compute) cuts it to $1\text{--}14\%$, beating matched-compute
bagging-$K{=}2$ on $8/9$ datasets and matching the $K{=}5$ result in
mean rank (Appendix~\ref{app:friedman}).  A single additional
bootstrap suffices to identify the top decile of fragile predictions,
which captures $68\text{--}94\%$ of the molecules retraining will flip
(Figure~\ref{fig:decile}); a triage workflow that routes only that
decile to a human reviewer is a one-bootstrap operation on top of
an existing pipeline.

\paragraph{Stability is a property of the procedure.}
A model's cross-sample churn is not a fact about the trained
weights; it is a fact about the procedure that produced them ---
the optimiser, the schedule, the data-resampling protocol.  In the
codistillation-vs.\ twin-indep comparison
(Appendix~\ref{app:overlap_spectrum}), a single hyperparameter on
the procedure --- the fraction of data the two networks share ---
separates a column that goes positive on DILI ($100\%$ overlap) from
one that never does ($\sim$$40\%$).  The same applies across
architectures: at the BACE-MLP-frozen $\lambda{=}300$ twin-indep
over-regularises GIN by $15$pp id-accuracy; the same selection rule
re-applied on BACE-GIN picks $\lambda{=}10$ and recovers the result
($-47.6\%$ argmax churn vs.\ ERM-GIN, Appendix~\ref{app:gin}); the
same rule re-applied on BACE-ChemBERTa and on Waterbirds also picks
$\lambda{=}10$ and recovers the result
(Appendices~\ref{app:chemberta_scope},~\ref{app:waterbirds_lambda}).
The selection rule transfers; the numerical $\lambda$ does not.
Multi-class output and $N$-aware $\lambda$-scaling are open
(Section~\ref{sec:scope}); regression results are reported in
Appendix~\ref{app:regression}.

\paragraph{Cross-sample fragility belongs alongside accuracy.}
A model's churn is procedural, but a benchmark report is what the
field reads.  Scientific-ML benchmark reports columns for accuracy,
AUROC, calibration, and (sometimes) worst-group accuracy.  None of
these varies the training dataset; cross-sample fragility does.
Two models trained on independent samples of the same population
that disagree on $16\%$ of test predictions while reporting
identical aggregate accuracy --- the BACE case
(Figure~\ref{fig:overview}) --- are indistinguishable in the report
column even though the practitioner sees the difference at the
next retraining.  Deep ensembles and MC dropout, the standard
parameter-side uncertainty methods, move argmax churn by $-4.6$ to
$+1.8$pp across the nine datasets (Table~\ref{tab:main}); they are
not substitutes for a report column that measures sensitivity to
the training sample.  The clinical-biostatistics literature already
reports per-individual stability intervals~\citep{TODO_riley2023};
the case for the same in scientific ML rests on the magnitudes we
measure (Section~\ref{sec:measurement}).

\paragraph{Adding the column.}
Bagging~\citep{TODO_breiman1996} and codistillation
\citep{TODO_anil2018_codistillation} at the right inter-network
overlap already exist; what this paper adds is a measurement
protocol that makes their effect on cross-sample fragility visible,
and a regime --- small-$N$ from-scratch scientific ML --- where the
gap they close dominates the rest of the report.  Aggregate accuracy
on a fixed test set measures the deployed model; cross-sample
fragility measures the procedure that will produce the next one.  On
the nine chemistry benchmarks where we measured both, the second
varies by $8\text{--}22$ percentage points across retrainings while
the first changes by less than one.  Reading only the accuracy
column is reading half the report.
