\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{What the methods buy.}
The intro's toxicity classifier loses class on $8\text{--}17\%$ of
test molecules between retrainings on independent bootstraps of the
same training population.  Bagging-$K{=}5$ cuts that to
$4\text{--}9\%$ at no accuracy cost (Table~\ref{tab:main}); twin-indep
at $\lambda{=}300$ cuts it further to $1\text{--}7\%$ at $2\times$-ERM
compute matched against bagging-$K{=}2$.  A single additional
bootstrap suffices to identify the top decile of fragile predictions,
which captures $68\text{--}94\%$ of the molecules retraining will flip
(Figure~\ref{fig:decile}); a triage workflow that routes only that
decile to a human reviewer is therefore a one-bootstrap operation on
top of an existing pipeline.

\paragraph{Cross-sample fragility belongs alongside accuracy.}
Scientific-ML benchmark reports columns for accuracy, AUROC,
calibration, and (sometimes) worst-group accuracy.  None of these
varies the training dataset; cross-sample fragility does.  Two
models trained on independent samples of the same population that
disagree on $16\%$ of test predictions while reporting identical
aggregate accuracy --- the BACE case
(Figure~\ref{fig:overview}) --- are indistinguishable in the report
column even though the practitioner sees the difference at the next
retraining.  Deep ensembles and MC dropout, the standard
parameter-side uncertainty methods, leave $-0.2$ to $+1.1$pp on
argmax churn (Table~\ref{tab:main}); they are not substitutes for a
report column that measures sensitivity to the training sample.
The clinical-biostatistics literature already reports per-individual
stability intervals~\citep{TODO_riley2023}; the case for the same in
scientific ML rests on the magnitudes we measure
(Section~\ref{sec:measurement}).

\paragraph{Stability is a property of the procedure.}
A model's cross-sample churn is not a fact about the trained
weights; it is a fact about the procedure that produced them --- the
optimiser, the schedule, the data-resampling protocol.  The
codistillation-vs.\ twin-indep comparison
(Appendix~\ref{app:overlap_spectrum}) makes this concrete: a single
hyperparameter on the procedure --- the fraction of data the two
networks share --- separates a method that catastrophically fails on
TADF from a method that does not.  The same applies across
architectures: at the BACE-MLP-frozen $\lambda{=}300$ twin-indep
over-regularises GIN by $15$pp id-accuracy; the same selection rule
re-applied on BACE-GIN picks $\lambda{=}10$ and recovers the result
($-47.6\%$ argmax churn vs.\ ERM-GIN, Appendix~\ref{app:gin}).  The
selection rule transfers; the numerical $\lambda$ does not.

\paragraph{What still blocks adoption.}
Three concrete gaps.  $(i)$~The recipe has one tuned
hyperparameter; an $N$-aware scaling $\lambda_N \propto 1/N$ is the
obvious follow-up for removing it but we have not verified the
scaling beyond BACE.  $(ii)$~Coverage is the small-$N$ from-scratch
regime; on pretrained-backbone settings the consistency loss
over-regularises features the backbone already encodes
(Section~\ref{sec:scope}).  $(iii)$~The fragility definition extends
unchanged to regression and multi-class (with an MAE or
covered-interval metric in place of class-flip rate), but we report
only binary classification here; matched-compute comparisons against
bootstrap-aware data augmentation, subbagging schedules, and
training-set diversity penalties are open.
